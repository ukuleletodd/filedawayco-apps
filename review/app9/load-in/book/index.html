The Promise

The Promise: You love music, stages and events. People get paid — well — to build them. This book shows you the whole world in plain language: how a show arrives on a truck, how the gear connects, what every job on the crew actually does, and how a beginner gets in. No experience assumed; nothing important skipped.

Chapter 1 — Why AV, Why Now

Every day, in every city in the country, somebody is paying to put on a show. A sales conference in a hotel ballroom. A wedding with a band. A product launch, a graduation, a church service with a full band and three cameras, a festival stage in a field. Behind every one of those events is a crew of people in black clothes who arrived before anyone else, built the whole thing out of cases and cable, ran it, tore it down, and left the room empty — and got paid a day rate for doing it.

That crew is the AV industry: audio, video, lighting. And it has a problem that is your opportunity — it is short of techs. Production companies and staffing agencies book more shows than they have reliable people for, which means the door for beginners is more open in this trade than in almost any other that pays this well without a degree.

What the work actually pays and how: most corporate AV work pays by the day or the call, not the hour — a set rate for showing up and doing your part of the show. And let's be honest about where a beginner actually starts, because it is not running anything. A beginner stagehand starts at the truck: unloading cases, pushing boxes, learning to wrap a cable properly, learning to build a screen, learning where things plug in. That is the real first rung, and it lasts longer than a day — it is your first month, and everybody senior to you did it too. The running comes later, in order: first you're trusted with pieces of a room, then with a room, then with a department, then with a board — and each step pays more, because each step is a skill somebody watched you prove. Nobody asks how long you've been around; they ask what you can do on the day. Chapter 9 lays out the whole ladder, rung by honest rung. Chapter 10 explains the shortcut that didn't exist five years ago — and why it only works standing on top of the slow rungs, not instead of them.

What the life is actually like, honestly: early calls — load-ins start when the room is free, and the room is often free at 6 a.m. Black clothes, head to toe; you are furniture that thinks. Long days on show days, quiet gaps between calls. And in exchange: backstage everywhere, music for a living, rooms most people only see from a seat, and the deep satisfaction of watching a bare ballroom become a show because your crew built it.

You do not need to be an engineer to start. You need to be reliable, teachable, and willing to push cases. The rest of this book is the teachable part.

Chapter 2 — A Show, Start to Finish

Every show you will ever work has the same skeleton. Learn the skeleton once and you can walk into any ballroom, theatre or festival site and know where you are in the day.

The Advance. Before anyone arrives, the show exists on paper: the production manager and the client have settled what the show is — the stage size, the screens, the mics, the lighting look, the schedule. The paperwork (the call sheet, the plot, the input list) is the show in advance. You will mostly meet the advance as a call time and an address. Know that it exists; the leads are working from it.

The Truck. Everything the show needs arrives in cases on a truck (Chapter 3).

Load-in. The gear comes off the truck, into the room, and gets built: stage, truss, speakers, screens, lights, cable everywhere. Load-in is the physical heart of the day and where beginners earn their keep.

Set and Focus. Built becomes aimed: speakers pointed and tested, projectors aligned to screens, lights focused on the stage, mics checked. This is where Chapters 4–6 live.

Sound Check / Rehearsal. The presenters and performers arrive. Levels get set, slides get clicked through, cues get rehearsed. The show stops being construction and becomes a performance with the audience missing.

Show Call. Doors open. The crew takes positions — board operators at their consoles, stagehands on headsets in the wings — and the show runs on cues (Chapter 8).

Strike. The audience leaves; the show comes apart in reverse — usually faster than it went up, because nobody focuses anything on the way out.

Load-out. Cases close, the truck loads, the room goes back to empty. The last push of the day, and then it is as if you were never there — which is exactly the point.

Hold that shape in your head. Every chapter after this one is a zoom into one part of it.

Chapter 3 — The Truck & Load-In

The gear lives in road cases: heavy black boxes on wheels, each one labelled, each one packed the same way every time so the next crew — maybe in another city — finds everything where it should be. Cases stack, roll in trains, and come off the truck down a ramp or a lift gate, across a dock plate, and into the room.

Your first jobs will be here, and they are not beneath anyone — every L1 in the business started pushing cases. The etiquette is simple and total:

  • Hands on the case means hands on the case: when a case is moving, you don't let go to check your phone.
  • Call your moves: "rolling," "stopping," "door" — the crew hears you before they see you.
  • Stack with the crew, lift with the crew. Heavy cases are team lifts, always. Nobody is impressed by a hero with a hurt back; a hurt back ends seasons.
  • Steel-toe boots where the call requires them (many do), gloves in your pocket, and never — ever — walk under a load in the air or stand where a falling case would land.
  • Learn the labels as you push: within a week of load-ins you'll know what a distro case, a cable trunk and a fixture case look like, and you'll start being sent for things by name. That is the first promotion, and it happens on the dock.
  • Two foundation skills get taught right here, in the load-in, and you should grab both chances the first time they're offered: wrapping cable (over-under, every time, until your hands do it without asking your brain — a badly wrapped cable is the fastest way to look new) and building screens (frame together, legs on, surface up, squared and dressed). These are beginner skills the way scales are beginner skills for a pianist: you will use them on your thousandth show exactly as on your first.

Load-in pace is set by the leads; your job is steady, not fast. A smooth load-in is quiet. If you can be the beginner who is always where the next case is going, you will be remembered — and remembered is how the ladder starts (Chapter 9).

Chapter 4 — Audio, Distilled

Honestly, there are not that many parts. Every audio system you will meet is one chain:

A SOURCE makes the sound → a CABLE carries it → the MIXER shapes and combines it → AMPLIFIERS make it powerful → SPEAKERS make it loud → ears hear it. Everything in audio is a variation on that chain. When sound is wrong, you walk the chain until you find the break. That is most of troubleshooting, for your whole career.

The Sources You'll Meet First

  • Handheld mic — for singers, speakers who like to move, Q&A runners. One in the hand, one spare, always.
  • Lavalier (lav) — the little clip-on mic for presenters who need their hands free. Clipping it on well (cable hidden, mic at the chest, no jewellery or scarf rubbing it) is an art, and it's the A2's art.
  • Podium/gooseneck mic — the fixed mic on the lectern. Bent right, it picks up a speaker who never thinks about mics at all.
  • Playback — a laptop or phone playing walk-in music, stingers and videos. Somebody's laptop is a source on almost every show.

Etiquette that matters: a mic is live until you KNOW it's muted. Treat every mic that way, on stage and backstage, forever.

The Mixer in one paragraph: every source gets a channel — a strip with a volume fader and tone controls. The mixer's job is balance: the presenter louder than the music, the music present but never fighting the words. Processing (the paragraph version): a little EQ to make voices clear, compression to even out a speaker who whispers then booms — that's most of it at this level; the A1's ears do the rest.

Feedback — the howl. It happens when a mic hears its own speaker and the loop builds. The three moves that kill it: (1) point the mic away from the speaker / move the speaker away from the mic — geometry first; (2) turn it down — the fader is always an honest answer; (3) ring it out — the A1 finds the offending frequency in EQ and notches it. You will hear feedback in your first month. Now you know it's a physics problem with three answers, not a disaster.

Line Check: before the show, every source gets tested one at a time, in order, and called out — "mic one, check." It's slow, it's ritual, and it is how show-time surprises get found at 2 p.m. instead of 7.

The A2's Day (preview of Chapter 9): mics ready, batteries fresh, RF (wireless) frequencies coordinated so twelve wireless mics don't fight each other, presenters mic'd and made comfortable, spare everything within reach. Invisible when perfect. It usually is.

Chapter 5 — Video, Distilled

The video chain is as simple as audio's: a SOURCE (a laptop with slides, a playback machine, a camera) → the SWITCHER chooses → the SCREEN shows it.

The Switcher's One Job: deciding what the room sees, moment to moment — the presenter's slides, then the camera's close-up of the presenter, then the video roll-in, back to slides. The V1 runs it like an editor live. The buttons you'll see — Program (what's live) and Preview (what's next) — are the whole concept: never take a source to Program you haven't seen in Preview.

Screens and Projectors in plain terms: a projector throws an image; brightness is measured in lumens and fights the room's light — a washed-out image is usually a bright room and an under-powered projector, not a broken one. Throw is the distance the projector needs for the image size; rear projection hides the projector behind the screen, front projection puts it in the room. Modern shows often skip projectors for LED walls and big displays — same chain, brighter box.

The Confidence Monitor: the screen at the stage's edge, facing the presenter, showing them what the audience sees (or their notes/timer). It's the presenter's security blanket, and setting it is in every breakout package in Chapter 7.

Cameras — the V2's world: frame the speaker (headroom, no chopped foreheads), focus and stay focused, follow smoothly — the audience should never notice a camera move. Record and playback duties often live here too.

Resolutions Without the Headache: content is built in shapes (16:9 is the standard you'll meet); the beginner's rule is match the source to the screen's shape and let the switcher/scaler handle the rest — and test the actual show laptop early, because the deck you rehearsed is never the deck that arrives.

Chapter 6 — Lighting, Distilled

Lighting is the department beginners find most mysterious and the one this book cares most about — because Chapter 10 turns it into your superpower. The parts, plainly:

Fixtures (the lights themselves)

  • Washes — broad, soft coverage; they light the stage evenly. The workhorse.
  • Profiles/spots — shaped beams with edges you can cut with shutters; for picking out a podium, a performer, a logo.
  • Movers (moving heads) — fixtures that pan, tilt, change colour and pattern under board control. The flashy ones — and just fixtures with more channels.
  • Uplights — small fixtures on the floor washing walls and drapes in colour. Half of corporate "look" is uplights.
  • House and practical lights — the room's own light and any on-stage lamps; somebody has to own them too, and it's lighting.

Truss: the aluminium structure fixtures hang from. Know what it is, know it goes up under the ME's and leads' direction, and know the beginner's rule: you help where you're put, and rigging — anything overhead — is never a beginner's improvisation (Chapter 10's honesty rules apply here with force).

Power, One Careful Page: lights eat power, power is distributed from a distro by people whose job it is (the ME), and the rule for everyone else is ASK BEFORE YOU PLUG ANYTHING IN. A circuit has a limit; the person who planned the distribution knows it and you don't. This page is short because the rule is total.

DMX — the one idea to carry into Chapter 10: DMX is how the board talks to the lights. Every fixture gets an ADDRESS — a number that says "these channels are yours" — and the board sends instructions down the cable by channel: intensity, colour, movement. Patching (Chapter 10's core skill) is simply telling the board which fixtures live at which addresses, so when you raise a fader, the right light answers. Hold that sentence; it's the key to the centrepiece.

The Board (console): faders, encoders, screens and buttons that turn DMX into a show. Every brand organises itself differently; every brand obeys the same logic: patch → build → play back. Different board, same job. That is why the next chapters matter more than any manual.

Chapter 7 — The Breakout Room

The general session is the big room — plenary, keynote, everyone together. Breakouts are the small rooms where the conference splits into sessions of twenty to a hundred people, and they are where beginners become techs, because a breakout room is a whole show small enough for one person to own.

The Standard Package (what rental houses and production companies spec, room after room): a screen or display; a projector; a small mixer; one or two speakers; a podium mic or a lav; a presentation laptop; and — where the room's size earns it — a confidence monitor. When you can set that package alone, you are employable anywhere in corporate AV — but notice the word "alone," because you won't start there. You start as the second pair of hands in somebody else's breakout room: you build the screen, you run and tape the cable, you swap the batteries, you hold the ladder of small tasks while the tech who owns the room runs the order below. You learn the order by doing its pieces, room after room, until one day the room is yours and a new beginner is doing the pieces for you. That is how this trade teaches everything — pieces first, whole thing after.

The Setup Order That Never Changes: screen/projector first (it decides the room's geometry) → speakers placed and aimed → mixer in position → mics → laptop and confidence monitor → cable runs taped and tidy → line check and click-through of the actual deck. Order matters because each step assumes the last; fight the order and you'll re-do work with a presenter watching.

Presenter-proofing — the part that makes you a professional: fresh batteries in the clicker and the lav, and spares in the room; the clicker TESTED from the presenter's spot, not from the tech table; tape over every cable a foot can find; a water-friendly distance between laptops and coffee; the first slide up on screen before the presenter arrives, so the room looks ready — because a ready-looking room calms presenters, and calm presenters give you an easy show.

Why It's the Best First Rung: a breakout tech owns a room end-to-end — set, check, run, strike — at a scale where mistakes are lessons instead of headlines. Agencies know this: good breakout techs get asked back by name, and "asked back by name" is the entire early game (Chapter 11). The deep system for climbing from here is Book Two's subject; this book's job is to get you into the room.

Chapter 8 — Running the Show

Show call is the part the audience sees — which means, done right, they never see you at all.

Cues: the show is a list of moments — lights up, video roll, mic live, walk-in music — each with a number or a name, each called by one voice: the stage manager or show caller. Operators don't guess; they take cues. Standby ("Standby LX 12") means hands on, eyes up. "GO" means go. That two-word discipline is the difference between a show and a scramble.

Comms (headsets): the crew's nervous system. Etiquette is short and absolute: say your name/position when you join ("Lighting on comms"); keep the channel for show business — chatter goes to the other channel or waits; answer a standby with your position ("LX standing by"); if you didn't hear, say so — a repeated cue is free, a missed one isn't. Appendix B has the phrases.

The Operator's Real Job is Anticipation: the A1 rides the presenter's mic before they get loud, the V1 has the next source in Preview before the cue, the L1's hand is on the fader before the room goes dark. Reaction is what the audience notices; anticipation is what they never do — which is the job.

Troubleshooting Calm: something will go wrong on your shows — a dead mic battery, a laptop that sleeps, a fixture that won't answer. The professional sequence never changes: the room must not know → swap to the spare/backup while the show continues → fix the broken thing after. Every department's spares (Chapter 4's spare handheld, Chapter 7's batteries) exist for this paragraph. Calm is a skill; crews promote calm.

Chapter 9 — The Roles & the Ladder

Every title on a call sheet, what the day actually looks like, and the one skill that unlocks the next rung. Read it as a map: you enter at the bottom and every rung is a skill, not a waiting period.

Stagehand — the true first rung, and it deserves its own honest description, because this is where every single person above you on this ladder started. The day: unloading the truck, pushing cases and boxes to where they belong, and learning the trade's foundation skills one at a time — how to wrap a cable over-under so it lies flat and lasts, how a screen goes together (frame, legs, surface, dressed and squared), where things plug in and why that order matters, how to tape a cable run so nobody trips and it comes up clean. None of it is glamorous and all of it is the job: a tech who wraps cable badly or builds a crooked screen gets spotted in an hour, and a stagehand who does these things right gets handed the next thing. The skills of this rung, concretely: truck work, case handling, cable wrapping, screen building, plug-in basics, taping, labelling, strike. UNLOCKS: being known as reliable — leads notice hands, and they promote the hands they trust to pieces of a room.

Breakout Tech — owns a breakout room end-to-end (Chapter 7): set, checks, runs the session, handles presenters. The day: solo responsibility at small scale. UNLOCKS: trust with a room.

GAV — General AV Tech: the all-rounder — sets any department's basics, floats between rooms, fills gaps, first call when something small breaks anywhere. The day: variety, and being useful in six places before lunch. UNLOCKS: breadth — the GAV sees every department and picks a direction.

A2 — Audio Assist: mics, RF/wireless coordination, batteries, stage audio, presenters. The day: backstage, headset on, spares ready (Chapter 4). UNLOCKS: the A1's trust — A2s who never miss a battery change get taught the console.

V2 — Video Assist: cameras, playback, records, confidence monitors. The day: framing speakers, rolling videos on cue, feeding the switcher clean sources (Chapter 5). UNLOCKS: switcher time — V1s are made from V2s who frame beautifully.

L2 — Lighting Assist: hangs and focuses fixtures, runs cable and DMX, supports the board op. The day: ladders, focus calls ("turn it left — good, lock it"), learning the rig by touching all of it. UNLOCKS: the board — and Chapter 10 is about what happens when the board is a stranger.

A1 — runs audio for the show: the mixer, the balance, the room's sound on their ears. Top of the audio ladder short of management.

V1 — runs video: switching and cameras; the room sees what the V1 decides.

L1 — runs lighting: the board. The room's entire look, live, from their faders.

These three are the OPERATOR rungs — the highest day rates a tech earns without managing, and the rates Chapter 10's skill defends: operators get rebooked because shows are safe with them.

ME — Master Electrician: owns power and the lighting system end-to-end — distribution, loads, safety. The department's engineering authority.

TD — Technical Director: the engineering brain of the whole show — systems design, signal flow across departments, the person problems escalate to when they're technical and big.

PM — Production Manager: the whole production — client, budget, crew, schedule, the advance (Chapter 2). The PM's show starts weeks before yours and ends after the invoice.

How the ladder actually works: nobody promotes you; leads and agencies rebook you at the next rung after watching you do pieces of it. The techs who climb fastest make the next job easy to give them — which is the entire subject of the next chapter.

Chapter 10 — AI on the Spot: The Board You've Never Seen

There is a moment in every AV tech's career that decides their rate.

The show is set. The room is dressed. And the lighting board — the console that runs every light in the room — is a model you have never touched. Maybe you've never even seen one. The lights are hung, powered and cabled, and they are dark, because between "the rig is built" and "the room is lit" stands one thing: somebody has to make the board talk to the lights.

The tech who can walk up to an unfamiliar board and get the lights working gets the L1 calls. The L1 calls are the top of the tech ladder — the highest day rates a working tech earns without becoming a TD or PM. The tech who can't, doesn't. For decades, the only bridge across that gap was months of manuals, classes, and slow shows next to someone patient.

That bridge is gone. This chapter is about the new one — and I know it works, because it has saved two of my shows.

Twice is not luck. Twice is a method. Here it is.

The Method — Five Moves, in Order

1. Photograph Before You Touch. Take clear pictures of the whole board and close-ups of every section — screens, faders, encoders, buttons. Video a slow pan. This is your map, and it's what you'll show the AI. A board you can see clearly is a board you can ask about precisely.

2. Ask What You're Looking at. Start with orientation, not commands: "This is a [board name]. I'm a lighting tech who needs to patch fixtures and get them on a fader. Walk me through the layout first." Make the AI teach you the board's logic — every console has one, and once you know where patching lives, the rest is steps.

3. One Step at a Time, Verified with Your Eyes. Never batch five instructions. Do the step, look at the board, confirm the result matches what the AI said should happen, then go on. When it doesn't match, photograph what you actually see and ask again with the new picture. The loop — photo, question, step, verify — is the whole skill.

4. Build One Working Thing First. Patch ONE fixture. Get it to respond. Put it on a fader and bring it up. A single light obeying you means the chain works — console, patch, DMX, fixture — and every light after it is repetition, not discovery.

5. Expand, Then Make It a Look. Patch the rest, assign your faders the way you like to run a show, and save. Now you're not the tech who survived the board; you're the tech running it.

The Same Loop, Every Department: the board is where this method pays most, but the loop is not lighting-only. An audio mixer you've never run, a video switcher with an unfamiliar layout, a playback machine with menus like a submarine — photograph, ask, one verified step, one working thing, expand. The departments in Chapters 4–6 each have a console-shaped problem at their centre, and the loop fits all of them. Learn it once; carry it everywhere.

The Rules That Keep This Honest (Read These Twice)

  • AI can be wrong. Your eyes outrank it. If the board in front of you disagrees with the answer, believe the board, photograph the disagreement, and ask again.
  • This method is for operating gear — boards, switchers, mixers, playback. It is NOT for the things that can hurt people: power distribution, rigging, truss loads, anything overhead. Those belong to the ME and the leads, always, no exceptions, no AI. If you're not sure which side of the line a task is on, it is on their side.
  • Don't fake it silently. The professional move is: solve it, then tell your lead what you did and how. Leads rebook techs who rescue shows; they blacklist techs who hide things. The goal was never to pretend you knew the board. The goal is that the room got light and the show went on.
  • Signal and time are real constraints. Download what you can in advance when you know the venue, keep the phone charged, and start the loop early — the method is fast, but it is not instant, and load-in clocks don't stop.

Why This Chapter Is the Middle of the Book

Everything before this chapter taught you the parts: the chains, the gear, the rooms, the roles. This chapter is what turns parts into rates. The industry does not pay top rates for knowing one board — boards change from show to show. It pays top rates for the tech who can make ANY board work, on the day, under pressure. A generation ago that tech was rare because the learning curve was brutal. A tech today, with a phone, this loop, and the nerve to try hard, can handle almost anything a ballroom or a stage throws at them.

I am not the exception. I am the proof of the method. Twice.

Chapter 11 — Getting Your First Calls

The honest, short version — the deep system is Book Two; this chapter gets you working.

Where Calls Come from: two doors, and most techs use both. (1) Staffing agencies — companies whose whole business is keeping a roster of techs and filling production companies' calls; you register, you get offered shifts, you accept the ones you can do well. (2) Production companies directly — the firms that own the shows; slower to enter, better once you're known. Around both: the techs you work beside, who will say your name in rooms you're not in — the industry's real hiring engine.

Your Profile: name, city, the departments you'll work, honest experience. Honest is the load-bearing word: a profile that claims L1 and delivers a beginner gets one call, ever. A profile that says "stagehand — truck, cable and screens solid, assisting in breakout rooms, learning lighting" gets booked at exactly that — and then Chapter 10 is how you surprise people upward, on the day, with your lead told after.

First-Show Etiquette, the whole list: arrive early (early is on time; on time is late). Black clothes, clean, no logos screaming. Tools in your pocket — [TODD TO ADD: his pocket list — the exact tools he carries]. Phone charged and then put away during work. Ask where to be; when you finish a task, find the lead and ask for the next one — never disappear, never sit in the room. Names: learn the leads' names and use them.

The Truth to End on: the fastest legal shortcut up this ladder is Chapter 10's skill, because it converts your weakest moment — "I've never seen this board" — into the story leads retell. But the shortcut only works on top of everything else in this book: the chains, the rooms, the etiquette, the honesty rules. AI gets the lights on. Character gets you called back.

Chapter 12 — The Gear Field Guide

One plain page each on the common sights. Learn to name them on sight and load-ins start assigning you better jobs.

Screens: projection screens (front/rear — the projector sits in front of or behind the fabric) and LED walls (panels that lock together into a glowing surface). The screen is the room's face; creases, crooked hangs and wrong aspects are visible to everyone, which is why video sets it first.

Projectors: brightness in lumens, throw distance for image size, lens shift to square the image without moving the box. A dim image in a bright room is physics, not failure — close the blinds or spec a bigger projector (not your call as a beginner; knowing WHY is).

Cameras: tripod, head, the camera itself, cable back to the switcher. Broadcast-style chains at big shows; a single camera on a stick at small ones. Framing and focus are the craft (Chapter 5).

Audio Mixers: channels in, faders, groups, outputs to speakers. Small shows: a compact mixer on the breakout cart. Big shows: a digital console with scenes and layers — Chapter 10's loop applies.

Video Switchers: inputs (laptops, cameras, playback), Preview and Program, transitions (cut, dissolve). The room sees Program; your next move lives in Preview.

Laptops & Playback: the presenter's deck is the show's most fragile source — test the actual machine, actual file, actual clicker, early. Playback laptops for music/video get the same respect as consoles.

Monitors: confidence (faces the presenter — their slides/notes/timer), program (shows the operator what's live), stage/foldback speakers in audio are also called monitors — context tells you which. Words in this trade repeat; the room disambiguates.

Mics: handheld, lavalier, podium/gooseneck, headset; wired vs wireless (RF). Batteries are a consumable and a religion (Chapter 4).

Speakers: mains (the audience's sound), delays (fill for deep rooms), foldback (the stage hears itself), subs (the low end you feel). Placement and aim are half of audio.

Lighting Fixtures: washes, profiles, movers, uplights (Chapter 6) — plus the humble work lights that let you see during load-in and must be OFF for the show.

Truss: aluminium structure, sticks and boxes, base plates and motors. Admire it, help build it under direction, never improvise it (Chapter 6).

Comms: base station, belt packs, headsets — the crew's voice (Chapter 8). If you touch one thing first on any show, learn your comms.

Cable, One Honest Page: XLR carries audio (and sometimes DMX); DMX cable carries lighting control; SDI and HDMI carry video; power cable is its own world with its own rules (ask first, Chapter 6). Coil cable properly — over-under, taped runs, no trippers — because cable is where beginners are judged fastest: tidy cable is a visible promise that the rest of your work is tidy too.

  • ☐ standby answered
  • ☐ spares within reach
  • ☐ the room never knows.

After

  • ☐ strike your department
  • ☐ cases closed and labelled
  • ☐ load-out until the truck doors shut or the lead releases you
  • ☐ thank the lead by name. That last line is not politeness — it is how rosters work (Book Two).

Appendix B — Comms Phrases

"[Position] on comms" — joining the channel.

"Standing by" — answering a standby cue with your position: "LX standing by."

"Go" — the only word that fires a cue; anything else is conversation.

"Say again" — the professional way to ask for a repeat; free to use, always.

"Copy" — understood.

"Hold" — stop the sequence; wait for the caller.

"Work note" — flagging a non-urgent issue for after the moment passes.

Names before requests: "Dave, LX — can I get a hand at the truss?" lands better than shouting into the channel.
