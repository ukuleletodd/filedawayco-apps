Chapter 1 — Why AV, Why Now

Every day, in every city in the country, somebody is paying to put on a show. A sales conference in a hotel ballroom. A wedding with a band. A product launch, a graduation, a church service with a full band and three cameras, a festival stage in a field. Behind every one of those events is a crew of people in black clothes who arrived before anyone else, built the whole thing out of cases and cable, ran it, tore it down, and left the room empty — and got paid a day rate for doing it.

That crew is the AV industry: audio, video, lighting. And it has a problem that is your opportunity — it is short of techs. Production companies and staffing agencies book more shows than they have reliable people for, which means the door for beginners is more open in this trade than in almost any other that pays this well without a degree.

What the work actually pays and how: most corporate AV work pays by the day or the call, not the hour — a set rate for showing up and doing your part of the show. And let's be honest about where a beginner actually starts, because it is not running anything. A beginner stagehand starts at the truck: unloading cases, pushing boxes, learning to wrap a cable properly, learning to build a screen, learning where things plug in. That is the real first rung, and it lasts longer than a day — it is your first month, and everybody senior to you did it too. The running comes later, in order: first you're trusted with pieces of a room, then with a room, then with a department, then with a board — and each step pays more, because each step is a skill somebody watched you prove. Nobody asks how long you've been around; they ask what you can do on the day. Chapter 9 lays out the whole ladder, rung by honest rung. Chapter 10 explains the shortcut that didn't exist five years ago — and why it only works standing on top of the slow rungs, not instead of them.

What the life is actually like, honestly: early calls — load-ins start when the room is free, and the room is often free at 6 a.m. Black clothes, head to toe; you are furniture that thinks. Long days on show days, quiet gaps between calls. And in exchange: backstage everywhere, music for a living, rooms most people only see from a seat, and the deep satisfaction of watching a bare ballroom become a show because your crew built it.

You do not need to be an engineer to start. You need to be reliable, teachable, and willing to push cases. The rest of this book is the teachable part.
